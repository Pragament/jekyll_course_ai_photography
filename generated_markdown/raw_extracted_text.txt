Understanding Combinations and Exposure:

The Exposure Triangle: Aperture, ISO, and Shutter Speed are interconnected. Changing one setting often requires adjusting another to maintain proper exposure.

Exposure Meter: Your camera's built-in light meter helps determine the "correct" exposure. Aim for a meter reading around 0 (or middle gray).

Reciprocity: If you decrease one setting (e.g., narrower aperture, lower ISO, faster shutter speed), you need to increase another (e.g., wider aperture, higher ISO, slower shutter speed) to maintain the same exposure level.

Creative Choices: "Correct" exposure is subjective and depends on your artistic intent. You might intentionally underexpose (darker) for mood or overexpose (brighter) for a high-key look.

Prioritizing Settings: Decide which aspect is most important for your shot:

Depth of Field (Aperture Priority Mode - Av or A): Choose aperture first to control DOF, camera adjusts shutter speed.

Motion (Shutter Priority Mode - Tv or S): Choose shutter speed first to control motion blur, camera adjusts aperture.

Full Control (Manual Mode - M): You control all three settings for complete creative freedom.

Example Scenarios & Combinations:

Bright Sunny Day (Landscape): Narrow aperture (f/11-f/16) for deep DOF, Low ISO (100) for best quality, Fast shutter speed (e.g., 1/250s) to balance exposure.

Portrait in Golden Hour: Wide aperture (f/2.8-f/4) for shallow DOF, Low ISO (100-200) for best quality, Medium shutter speed (e.g., 1/125s) to balance exposure.

Indoor Low Light (Event): Wide aperture (f/2-f/2.8) to gather light, Higher ISO (800-3200) to increase sensitivity, Medium shutter speed (e.g., 1/60s - 1/125s) (consider using flash or image stabilization).

Fast Action Sports (Daylight): Medium aperture (f/4-f/5.6) for reasonable DOF, Low ISO (100-400) for good quality, Very fast shutter speed (e.g., 1/1000s - 1/4000s+) to freeze motion.

Night Photography (Cityscape): Narrow aperture (f/8-f/11) for sharpness, Low ISO (100-400) for less noise, Slow shutter speed (e.g., seconds to minutes) for long exposure (tripod essential).

7. Negative Prompt

Negative prompting allows precise control over colors and content. While the main prompt shapes the general image, negative prompts refine it by filtering out unwanted elements, textures, or hues, helping to achieve a focused, polished result. This enables more control over the final image, ensuring that distractions are minimized and that the output aligns closely with your intended vision.
